K-means playground

K-means finds groups of similar observations. You choose how many groups to look for (k). On this page, dots represent observations and ringed crosses represent group centres. Watch the dots join their nearest centre, then the centres move to their groups’ average positions. These steps repeat until the group assignments stop changing.

Steps on how K-means works
  1. Choose k: Pick the number of clusters (groups) you want to find in your dataset.
  2. Initialise centroids: Choose k starting centres. In this playground, they are selected randomly from the observations when enough points are available.
  3. Assign points: Assign each observation to its nearest centroid using Euclidean distance—the straight-line distance on this plot.
  4. Update centroids: Move each centroid to the average (mean) position of the points assigned to it.
  5. Repeat: Alternate assignment and movement until the group assignments stop changing. This is called convergence.

The maths, made simple
1 / 4
Distance · two measurementsExplore the formulas

How far is a dot from a centre?

This is the simplified 2D version of Euclidean distance. K-means measures the straight-line distance from each dot to every centroid. The nearest centre gets the dot.

d=(x−cx)2+(y−cy)2
(x, y) · the dot’s position(cx, cy) · the centre’s position

Find the horizontal and vertical gaps. Square each gap, add them, then take the square root.

A quick example

Dot (2, 3) → centre (5, 7)
The gaps are 3 across and 4 up.

d = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5
Repeat for each centre. The smallest distance wins—the green line in the calculation.

And SSE? It adds up these distances squared, using each dot’s assigned centre.

Distance with any number of features

The previous formula used just x and y. This general version works with n measurements per observation.

d(x,c)=∑i=1n(xi−ci)2
n · number of featuresi · feature number∑ · add the terms

For each feature, subtract the centre’s value from the dot’s value. Square, add and take the square root—exactly as before.

From 2D to 3D

With three features, suppose the differences are 3, 4 and 12.

d = √(3² + 4² + 12²) = 13
For n = 2, the sum has only two terms: our original horizontal and vertical gaps.

Why squared distance? Skipping the square root gives the same nearest-centre ranking. K-means minimises squared distances. Features with larger numerical scales can dominate, so scaling matters.

Centroid update: find the average

After assigning points, move each centre to the mean of the points in its group. Calculate the average of each feature separately.

c=1m∑r=1mxr
m · points in this groupxr · one point’s feature valuesc · the updated centre

Add the group’s point coordinates and divide by how many points it contains. Repeat for every group.

Three dots, one centre

Points: (2, 2), (4, 2), (6, 8)

cx = (2 + 4 + 6) ÷ 3 = 4
cy = (2 + 2 + 8) ÷ 3 = 4
The centre moves to (4, 4). It does not have to sit on a data point.

Why the mean? It minimises the group’s sum of squared distances for its current assignments. If a group has no points, this playground keeps its previous centre.

SSE: add up the squared distances

SSE measures the total fit of all points to their assigned centres. It is the quantity K-means tries to minimise.

SSE=∑p=1Ndp2
N · all points in the datasetdp · point p’s distance to its assigned centre

Square each assigned distance, then add them all. Do not take a square root at the end.

A quick example

Three points are 2, 3 and 5 units from their assigned centres.

SSE = 2² + 3² + 5² = 38
Lower SSE means a tighter fit on the same data and scale. It does not prove the groups are meaningful.

Putting it together: assign to the nearest centre → move to the group mean → repeat until assignments stop changing. These steps do not increase SSE in exact arithmetic, but can reach a local solution.

Choose, compare, explain

How many clusters should we choose?

K-means is a partition-based method: it divides observations into k non-overlapping groups, each represented by a centroid.

Choosing k changes the grouping. Compare several values on the same observations and feature scales. Lower SSE alone is not enough: more centres usually reduce error, even when the extra groups do not help answer your question.

Try it: choose a fixed example and change k. Watch the groups, look for diminishing SSE improvements, then compare silhouette scores. These examples are separate from the drawing playground above.

Two numerical features are standardised once for each example. The same fitted results are reused when you revisit a value of k.

Selected grouping

Compare the evidence

1 · Look for an elbow

The elbow is a bend where extra clusters give smaller SSE improvements. It can suggest a useful compromise, but a clear elbow may not exist. It is not an automatic proof of the correct k.

2 · Check silhouette

Silhouette compares closeness to an observation’s own group with separation from the nearest other group. The mean ranges from −1 to 1: larger values indicate better separation under the chosen distance. It is undefined for k = 1.

3 · Use context and inspect the plot

Compare the plots and scores with what a useful group would mean. Try the rings: a favourable score can still describe a grouping that misses the ring-shaped structure you care about.

How is silhouette calculated?

For observation i, a(i) is the mean distance to the other observations in its own cluster. b(i) is the smallest mean distance to a different cluster. Its silhouette is s(i) = [b(i) − a(i)] / max[a(i), b(i)]. The reported score is the mean across observations.

A score near 1 indicates strong separation, near 0 suggests overlap, and a negative score can suggest a poor assignment under this distance measure. A singleton cluster receives a score of 0. The calculation needs at least two groups and fewer groups than observations.

The examples use Euclidean silhouette and K-means with 20 initialisations per k. A fitted run can reach a local solution; repeated starts help but do not guarantee the global minimum.

A first look at Gaussian mixture models

What if groups overlap?

K-means assigns each observation to one nearest centroid. A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) models the data as a mixture of Gaussian components, each with a mean, covariance and weight. It can describe different spreads and orientations and estimate an observation’s membership probabilities.

Try it: switch between K-means and GMM on the same observations. Select a point to compare its hard assignment with its GMM probabilities.

Click any point to see its membership probabilities and K-means assignment. You can also focus a point and press Enter, or use the observation menu.

Membership is not always certain.

Cyan = component 1; magenta = component 2. A blended colour shows a mixture of membership probabilities. Component numbers are arbitrary, not known class labels.

Hard versus soft assignment

K-means returns a single group assignment. A GMM can give probabilities such as 55% and 45%. Choosing the largest probability produces a hard assignment too, but hides that ambiguity.

How does a GMM learn?

Typically, expectation–maximisation alternates between estimating memberships and updating component means, covariances and weights. This introduction focuses on interpreting the result; it does not derive the algorithm.

Probabilities still need interpretation

The probabilities depend on the fitted Gaussian model and its assumptions. They are not verified real-world class probabilities. A GMM is not automatically better, and the number of components still needs consideration.
